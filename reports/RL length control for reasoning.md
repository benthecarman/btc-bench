# Make correct answers pay for their length

The 2025–2026 research points to one design for our next run: add a **correct-only, multiplicative, group-relative length factor to the reward, scaled by the group's solve rate, and leave the loss as it is**. Three findings support it. First, accuracy against response length is an inverted U. Unconstrained RL overshoots the peak, and the extra length lands mostly in failing answers ([Nohara et al., Jun 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09591v3); [Su et al., Apr 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00127v1)). That is what our held-out eval showed after step 28. Second, our loss already has the fixes the literature prescribes for loss-side length bias: ScaleRL's "prompt-average" aggregation and Dr. GRPO-style removal of the per-response 1/|o| and std terms. Changing the loss will not stop this drift ([ScaleRL, Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786); [Dr. GRPO, Mar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)). Third, the 2026 work identifies penalizing the length of *wrong* answers under GRPO as the main route to collapse ([Acoer, Jun 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22716)). Multiplicative correct-only rescaling cut tokens by 40% and raised accuracy ([GR³, Mar 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10535)). Our own rollout logs change how rl27 should be read. The run was not uncapped for its first 24 steps. A 32,768-token rollout budget truncated 14% of rollouts and scored them 0, which acted as an implicit length penalty. After that budget was removed at batch 25, the median correct answer within mixed groups grew 2.4× (15.9k → 38.1k tokens), and failing answers grew about as fast. Nothing in the gradient pushed back. The recommended term fits the no-generation-cap rule because nothing is ever truncated and every correct answer still outscores every wrong one: the solvable tail pays a tax but is never censored.

Throughout this report, **"the sources show"** marks published results with links. **"Our measurement"** marks numbers we computed from `runs/rl27/batches/batch-{1..40}.jsonl` and `runs/rl27/config.json`. **"Our inference"** marks reasoning that no source tests directly.

## rl27 lost its only length brake when the 32k budget came off

**Our measurement.** The run notes in `runs/rl27/config.json` say that until batch 24, rollouts had a 32,768-token budget, "against the no-caps rule". From batch 25 there was no budget, and rollouts ended at EOS or at the 131,072-token server context. Across batches 1–24, **217 of 1,536 rollouts (14%) were truncated, and every one of them scored 0**. Optimize tasks account for 109 of them. Grouping the training rollouts by batch shows what happened once that pressure was gone:

| Batches | Rollout budget | Truncated / context hits | Median length, all rollouts | Mixed groups: correct median | Mixed groups: incorrect median | Mixed groups where wrong mean > correct mean |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 20–24 | 32,768 | 51 of 320 | 15.5k | 14.6k | 23.4k | 15 / 22 |
| 25–28 | none | 1 | 14.7k | 15.9k | 23.5k | 14 / 22 |
| 29–32 | none | 0 | 20.1k | 20.9k | 30.3k | 8 / 20 |
| 33–36 | none | 0 | 26.9k | 25.7k | 34.4k | 6 / 21 |
| 37–40 | none | 6 | 32.2k | 38.1k | 45.5k | 14 / 21 |

Two facts in this table matter for the design. First, **correct answers in mixed groups lengthened about as fast as wrong ones** (2.4× against 1.9×). The eval-time picture, with solved answers at 17–23k and unsolved at 42–64k at step 40, reflects which prompts the model solves more than a lengthening confined to failures. Second, **after the budget came off, the reward carried almost no within-group length signal**. Across batches 25–40, the wrong answers averaged longer than the right ones in only **42 of 84 mixed groups**. Before that, truncation turned "too long" into "wrong", so long rollouts in mixed groups received negative advantage. That is what Skywork-OR1 deliberately keeps: it found that giving truncated samples negative advantage "improves token efficiency" and preserves length scaling ([Skywork-OR1, May 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.22312)).

**Our inference.** The peak at step 28, three steps after the budget was removed, followed by length doubling, matches a run losing its only counter-pressure. It does not prove causation. Under the budget, the training median also rose from about 3k to about 15k (batches 6–24), partly because the task mix moved toward harder tiers after step 4. With only about 5 informative groups per step, the 40-step curve is also noisy.

**Our measurement, continued.** Looping exists but is a minority failure. Heavy repetition, defined as more than 20% of 32-token sequences repeated, appears in **11 of 439 incorrect rollouts of 32k tokens or more, and in 0 of 130 correct ones**. It concentrates late: about 10% of failures over 40k tokens in batches 36–40. Most long failures are therefore not loops, so a repetition penalty alone would not have saved the run. Short answers under 8k tokens show high repeated-sequence rates even when correct (14%), presumably from structured script output. Any repetition penalty must therefore ignore short answers.

## Accuracy peaks at an intermediate length, and the excess goes to failures

**The sources show** a consistent inverted U. Nohara, Nakamura and Yokota compared GRPO, DAPO, RLOO-LP, ALP and DRPO on 1.5–4B models. Accuracy "rises with length, reaches a peak, and then plateaus or declines". The best length-controlled Qwen3-4B-Base policy scored **24.7% on AIME24 at about 6.6k tokens, against 19.5% for the unconstrained baseline at more than 12k**. They attribute this to "mode leakage": the modal answer keeps improving with length while individual samples scatter from it ([arXiv 2602.09591, v3 Jun 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09591v3)). Incorrect answers are routinely 2–3× longer than correct ones: 11,519 against 3,514 tokens for R1-Distill-1.5B on MATH500 ([Fatemi et al., Apr 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.05185v3)), and over 6,000 against under 3,000 on MATH. In more than 60% of questions the shortest sampled answer was already correct ([Su et al., Apr 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.00127v1)). Forcing more thinking first helps and then hurts by raising output variance ([Ghosal et al., Jun 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.04210)). Longer reasoning degrades performance on distractor and constraint-tracking tasks ([Gema et al., Jul 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2507.14417)). Looping comes from risk aversion and temporally correlated errors, and temperature is only "a stopgap" ([Pipis et al., Dec 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2512.12895)).

The counterpoint deserves a fair hearing. Magistral finds that "reward scales logarithmically with the output length" and treats length growth as the engine of improvement ([Magistral, Jun 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.10910)). Its evidence covers 1.5–8k tokens, however, and Mistral still raised the cap in stages and kept a soft overlong penalty. DAPO says length growth gives "a larger space for exploration" but must be "jointly monitor[ed] with validation accuracy" ([DAPO, Mar 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476)). JustRL saw length *fall* from about 8k to 4–5k with no penalty while AIME24 rose to 55% ([JustRL, Dec 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16649)). Length is not a progress signal in either direction.

**Our inference.** rl27 sits far outside the studied regime: 24–49k-token medians, a 27B model, and a Bitcoin Script domain. Every paper here is math or code at 32k tokens or less, mostly at 1.5–7B. The inverted U and failure-heavy lengthening are robust enough across models that we should expect them, but the location of our peak is unknown. The decline from 0.491 to 0.434 alongside doubling length is the signature these papers describe. Because the run had only 80 tasks × 2 samples, the decline itself is only moderately certain (P(better) = 0.05 for step 40 against step 28).

## Our loss is already de-biased, so the fix must live in the reward

**The sources show** that the original GRPO objective's per-response 1/|o_i| makes "longer responses penalized less" when the advantage is negative, which inflates wrong answers. Dr. GRPO removes that term and the std divisor ([Dr. GRPO, Mar 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.20783)). DAPO's token-level loss reaches the same length-independent per-token weight from the other side ([DAPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.14476)). ScaleRL compared sample-, prompt- and token-average aggregation, and **prompt-average had the best asymptotic performance**. Prompt-average normalizes each prompt's tokens by 1/Σ_g|y_g| with equal weight per prompt, which is exactly our `len(kept) * group_tokens` denominator. ScaleRL also found prompt-level std, batch-level std and no std "yield similar performance", that zero-variance filtering helps, and that its CISPO-style truncated-IS REINFORCE beat DAPO ([ScaleRL, Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.13786)). That is the same family as our loss. On 2026 refinements: "Balanced Aggregation" shows that per-group token-mean couples sign and length, so when negatives are systematically longer it overweights them. It fixes this by averaging positives and negatives separately ([arXiv 2605.04077, May 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2605.04077)). MiniMax-M1 saw negatives lengthen faster than positives and hit the context limit first ([MiniMax-M1, Jun 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13585)).

**Our inference.** Within a group, our per-token weight is Â_i/(N·T_g). That is the unbiased REINFORCE gradient up to one shared constant, so the GRPO "verbose failure" artifact survives only as a diluted leak. T_g includes each response's own length, roughly 1/G as strong as GRPO's bias. Balanced Aggregation's coupling is weak for us because wrong-longer-than-right was a coin flip in batches 25–40. Neither mechanism is large enough to explain a 2× drift, and no source finds that switching aggregation controls length at our scale. **Keep the loss as it is.** Three implementation details do matter once a length term goes into the reward.

**Do not add per-group std normalization.** A length factor gives all-correct groups non-zero variance. Dividing by their tiny std would make 5% length differences as loud as correctness, which is the divergence pathway Acoer blames for collapse ([Acoer](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22716)). Our no-std advantage keeps the length signal at its natural, small size. **Fix the per-prompt weight at 1/8.** The current 1/len(kept) lets newly informative all-correct groups dilute the correctness signal of mixed groups. **Change the out-of-memory skip.** The code in `rl/local/train.py` (`train_on`) drops the skipped rollout's gradient but keeps its tokens in `group_tokens` and its reward in the group mean. Skipped rollouts are the longest, and probably failures. Dropping only them acts as a length-selective "overlong filter". It removes the strongest push against long failures, and the surviving advantages no longer sum to zero (the sum is likely positive). Dropping the whole group and logging it removes that bias.

The truncated-IS cap of 2 is not length-coupled, because it is per token with no 1/|o| term. In rl27 it corrected both NVFP4-versus-bf16 mismatch (17% of tokens fell outside a 0.2 clip from mismatch alone, per config) and up to two steps of adapter staleness. It is fine as is.

## Correct-only, solve-rate-scaled penalties are the 2026 consensus

**The sources show** five families of length-aware reward. They are: per-prompt normalized penalties among correct samples ([Arora & Zanette, Feb 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.04463)); group-relative targets ([Kimi k1.5, Jan 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12599), [ShorterBetter, Apr 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2504.21370), [GR³, Mar 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10535)); budget or truncation rewards ([ThinkPrune](https://arxiv.org/abs/2504.01296), [L1](https://arxiv.org/html/2503.04697), [DLER, Oct 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15110)); difficulty-adaptive penalties ([ALP, Jun 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05256), [GRPO-λ, May 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18086), [Laser-D/DE, May 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2505.15612)); and filtering with no reward term ([GFPO, Aug 2025](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2508.09726)). Typical reported trade-offs are 35–80% fewer tokens for 0 to −4 accuracy points, and several report gains. The late-2025 and 2026 work has sharpened which shapes are safe:

| Method (date) | Length signal | Who pays | Reported effect | Fits no-cap rule? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| GR³ (Mar 2026) | R·1/(1+α·ℓ/ℓ̄), α = 0.33, ℓ̄ = group mean | Correct only (wrong already 0) | R1-Distill-7B AIME24: 13.2k → 7.9k tokens, 57.1 → 60.1 | Yes |
| Acoer (Jun 2026) | 1 + α_t·g(ℓ/B_t), adaptive α and budget | Correct only | Qwen3-1.7B MATH-500: −62% tokens at equal accuracy; GRPO+LP, GRPO-LEAD, ReCut collapsed 100% | Yes |
| DRPO (Oct 2025) | Length weights normalized within correct subgroup | Correct only | GSM8K −77% tokens, −1.1 pt (RLOO-LP: −68%, −4.3) | Yes |
| ALP (Jun 2025) | −β·ℓ·max(p_solved, 1/K), β = 1e-7 | All, scaled by solve rate | ~−50% tokens; +2 AIME, +4 Olympiad, −1 MATH500 | Mostly (1/K floor taxes unsolved prompts) |
| GRPO-λ (May 2025) | Penalty only in high-accuracy groups | Correct, gated | −47% length, +1.48 avg | Yes |
| Kimi k1.5 (Jan 2025) | Min-max λ ∈ [−0.5, 0.5] in group; wrong get min(0, λ) | Both | 60.8 AIME24 at 3.3k tokens (long2short) | Yes, but penalizes long wrong answers |
| DAPO / Magistral ramp (2025) | Linear to −1 (DAPO) or −0.1 (Magistral) over last L_cache tokens | All, near a soft limit | +3 AIME in DAPO ablation; ScaleRL: "does not improve performance" | Only at small magnitude |
| ThinkPrune / DLER (2025) | Zero reward past a target length | Both | DLER −69–77% length, accuracy "maintained" | **No**: a cap moved into the reward |
| L1 (Mar 2025) | Target length in the prompt ± deviation | Both | ~3% length deviation | **No**: caps plus prompt-surface change |

Three lessons stand out. First, **penalizing wrong-answer length is the main collapse risk**. A continuous length penalty on wrong answers puts "constant downward pressure" on failures, and in all-wrong groups std normalization blows up the advantages ([Acoer](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22716)). Under additive penalties, a correct-but-long answer can fall below the group mean and be pushed *down* ([DRPO](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.04474v1)), and "additive penalties introduce a compensatory effect" that the policy can exploit ([GR³](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.10535)). The opposite mistake, rewarding long wrong answers as the cosine reward does, produced repetition hacking that needed an n-gram penalty ([Yeo et al., Feb 2025](https://arxiv.org/html/2502.03373)). Second, **difficulty-aware allocation is the most credible source of accuracy gains**. ALP spends 21% of tokens on the easiest half of problems and keeps hard ones long ([ALP](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.05256)), and gating the penalty by group accuracy avoids "premature accuracy collapse" ([GRPO-λ](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.18086)). Third, **the penalty's shape matters less than the optimizer around it**. DLER finds that truncation, cosine, L1-Max and Laser all "shift performance along the efficiency frontier rather than beyond it" once normalization and sampling are fixed ([DLER](https://arxiv.org/html/2510.15110)). Nohara et al. find no method gives uniform accuracy gains, and say strength must be swept per model ([arXiv 2602.09591](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09591v3)).

Timing evidence points the same way. Kimi found the length penalty "may slow down training during initial phases" and introduced it only after a warmup, then held it constant ([Kimi k1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12599)). In JustRL, an overlong penalty applied from the start collapsed entropy from 1.2–1.4 to 0.5–0.6 and cost 5–10 AIME points ([JustRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16649)). Most large capability-RL runs (Skywork-OR1, AceReason, POLARIS, MiniMax-M1) used *no* explicit length penalty and instead relied on staged hard caps ([MiniMax-M1](https://arxiv.org/html/2506.13585); [POLARIS](https://hkunlp.github.io/blog/2025/Polaris/)). Our rule forbids that tool, which is why the reward has to carry the pressure.

**Our inference on the rule.** A length term is compatible with "no generation caps" if two conditions hold. Generation is never stopped early: no truncation, no forced "time is up" interruption as in ScaleRL, and no MiniMax-style early stop. And **every correct answer still outscores every wrong answer in its group**, so a long correct solution is taxed but never scored like a failure. Multiplicative correct-only rescaling satisfies both for any finite length. Truncation rewards, L1 budgets and a full-strength DAPO ramp fail the second condition, which makes them caps in disguise. Gating the strength by solve rate adds one more protection: prompts the model rarely solves, where the long solvable tail lives, get almost no length pressure, and all-wrong groups get none.

## A prioritized plan for the next run

**Start from base, not from step 28.** rl27's first 24 steps ran under a 32k budget, so its step-28 adapter is not a rule-compliant starting point, and the comparison would be confounded. Resuming from step 28 with the length term is a cheaper diagnostic if compute is tight. That is Kimi's "warm up without penalty, then switch on" structure ([Kimi k1.5](https://arxiv.org/html/2501.12599)), but it cannot produce headline numbers. Keep everything else from rl27 so the length term is the only change: 8 prompts × 8 rollouts, temperature 1.0 / top-p 0.95 / top-k 20, LoRA rank 32, lr 1e-4, truncated-IS REINFORCE with cap 2, no KL, one optimizer step per batch, and context-limit rollouts scored 0.

For group g with rollouts i = 1..8, benchmark rewards r_i ∈ [0, 1], response lengths ℓ_i, group mean length ℓ̄_g and group mean reward p̂_g, the training reward is:

```
α_g   = α · p̂_g                                   # α = 0.33 after warmup
r̃_i  = r_i · (1 + α_g) / (1 + α_g · ℓ_i / ℓ̄_g)    # wrong answers stay exactly 0
Â_i   = r̃_i − mean_g(r̃)                           # no std division
weight per token = Â_i / (8 · T_g)                 # fixed 1/8 per prompt
```

The (1 + α_g) numerator is our addition to GR³. It scores a mean-length correct answer at exactly 1, so the correctness gradient keeps rl27's scale. At full strength, a correct answer at twice the group mean scores 0.80 and one at half the mean scores 1.14. In a group with 1 of 8 correct, α_g ≈ 0.04 and the factor is within ±4%. `score` and all reports stay the unchanged benchmark number. This is a trainer-side term, as DESIGN.md already plans, and no prompt text changes.

**Monitoring (our design, all derivable offline from `batch-N.jsonl`).** Every step, log the median and p90 length of correct and incorrect answers *within mixed groups*, and the share of mixed groups where wrong answers average longer than right ones. Log the counts of mixed, all-correct and all-wrong groups. Log the ratio of mean |length component| to mean |correctness component| of the advantage, and keep it at or below about 0.2, in line with Magistral's 10% bound. Log the heavy-repetition share in long failures, context hits, and OOM skips. Finally, log the mean sampled-token negative log-probability from `rollout_logprobs` as a cheap entropy proxy, because entropy collapse was how the penalty failed in [JustRL](https://arxiv.org/html/2512.16649).

Track two alarms against the steps 6–10 reference. Drift: correct-answer median in mixed groups above 1.5× the reference. Over-compression: below 0.5× the reference while the training solve rate falls. On an over-compression alarm, or two consecutive held-out declines with P(drop) ≥ 0.8 from `decide.py`, halve α. This is a coarse version of Acoer's accuracy-guarded control loop ([Acoer](https://arxiv.org/html/2606.22716)).

**Checkpoint selection.** Pick the best checkpoint by held-out score. Picking the best of about 8 evals inflates the winner, so confirm the pick with a fresh third sample before reporting it. Because responses are stored, also compute majority-vote agreement against pass@1 on existing eval samples. Nohara et al.'s mode-leakage finding predicts that sample accuracy falls before the modal answer does ([arXiv 2602.09591](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.09591v3)). A success criterion for a 40-step run: held-out scores after step 28 do not fall significantly below the best checkpoint, and eval median length stays near the 17–24k range of base and step 28 instead of climbing toward 49k.

**What remains uncertain.** α = 0.33 is GR³'s calibration for 7B math at about 13k tokens, not ours, and the literature says strength must be swept. A second arm at α = 0.15 is the most informative extra run if compute allows. No source tests length control above 32B, beyond 32k tokens, or on a domain like Bitcoin Script. It is also untested whether correct-only pressure shortens long *failures* on prompts the model never solves. GR³ and Acoer report overall token reductions, but our failures on hard prompts may keep growing until the context fills, and they would then score 0 with no gradient. With about 5 informative groups per step, noise in the length signal is high. Doubling prompts per step would help more than any penalty refinement, if throughput allows.

## Conclusion

The new reading of rl27 is that it was a controlled experiment by accident. Truncation-as-failure kept length in check, and its removal let length drift with no opposing gradient. Correct and wrong answers grew together, and the reward–length correlation inside groups fell to a coin flip. That makes the problem simpler than "the loss is biased" or "the model learned to overthink". The reward lost a term, and the job is to put one back in a form the no-cap rule allows. The literature's most important 2026 contribution is the rule for which form: make the length price fall only on answers that are already correct, scale it by how easy the prompt is, and keep std normalization away from it. Under that rule, a long correct solution to a hard prompt costs a few percent of reward, never all of it. That is the same honest-measurement principle that motivated banning caps, applied to the reward.
